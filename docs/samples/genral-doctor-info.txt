# General Medicine Reference Guidelines

This document provides general, doctor-approved medical information for common
conditions seen in a general medicine practice. It is intended as a reference
for patient consultation only and does not replace individual clinical
judgment.

---

## Common Cold

The common cold is a mild viral infection of the nose and throat. Symptoms
typically include sneezing, a runny or blocked nose, a sore throat, mild
cough, and sometimes a low-grade fever. Symptoms usually peak within 2 to 3
days and resolve on their own within 7 to 10 days.

Management is supportive: rest, adequate fluid intake, and warm fluids help
relieve symptoms. Saline nasal drops and throat lozenges provide symptomatic
relief. Cold symptoms typically do not require antibiotics, because the
illness is caused by a virus.

A patient with cold symptoms that last longer than 10 days, worsen
significantly, or are accompanied by a high fever should consult a doctor.

---

## Fever

A fever is a temporary rise in body temperature, most often to 100.4 F (38 C)
or higher. Fever is a normal immune response to an infection and is usually
not dangerous by itself. Common causes include viral and bacterial infections.

Mild fever in an otherwise healthy adult can be managed at home with rest and
fluids. If discomfort is significant, the patient may use fever-reducing
medication at the lowest effective dose. Fever with a rash, neck stiffness,
severe headache, confusion, or difficulty breathing requires urgent medical
evaluation.

For young children and infants, fever should be evaluated more conservatively.
An infant under 3 months with a fever of 100.4 F (38 C) or higher should be
evaluated by a doctor promptly.

## Headache

Headache is one of the most common complaints in general medicine. Most
headaches are primary headaches, meaning they are not caused by another
medical condition.

Tension headaches are the most common type. They present as bilateral,
pressing or tightening pain of mild to moderate intensity, often felt like a
band around the head. They are not usually aggravated by routine physical
activity and are not associated with nausea or vomiting.

Migraine is characterized by recurrent unilateral throbbing headache, lasting
4 to 72 hours, often accompanied by nausea, sensitivity to light
(photophobia), and sensitivity to sound (phonophobia). Some people experience
a visual or sensory aura before the attack begins.

Red flags that warrant urgent evaluation include sudden onset thunderclap
headache, headache with fever and neck stiffness, new neurological deficit,
headache in patients with cancer or pregnancy, and headache beginning after
age 50.

For an acute tension headache, over-the-counter pain relief at the lowest
effective dose may be used. Patients with frequent tension headaches should
keep a headache diary, identify triggers, and maintain regular hydration and
sleep. Chronic headache disorders greater than 15 days per month should be
reviewed by a doctor.

## Hypertension (High Blood Pressure)

Hypertension is defined as persistently elevated blood pressure, generally a
reading of 130/80 mmHg or higher on repeated measurements. It is often
asymptomatic, which is why it is sometimes called the silent killer.

Uncontrolled high blood pressure increases the risk of heart attack, stroke,
kidney damage, and vision problems. Management focuses on lifestyle measures:
reducing salt intake, maintaining a healthy weight, regular physical
activity, limiting alcohol, and avoiding tobacco.

Medications used to treat hypertension include ACE inhibitors, angiotensin
receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, and diuretics. The choice of
medication is individualized. A patient with a blood pressure reading above
180/120 mmHg with symptoms such as severe headache, chest pain, or vision
change requires urgent care.

## Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body becomes resistant to
insulin or does not produce enough insulin, leading to elevated blood sugar.

Common risk factors include obesity, physical inactivity, family history of
diabetes, and age above 45. Many patients are asymptomatic initially, which
is why routine screening is important. Symptoms, when present, may include
increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and blurred vision.

Management includes weight loss when needed, a balanced diet with controlled
carbohydrates, regular physical activity, and blood sugar monitoring.
Medication such as metformin is commonly the first-line oral agent.
Complications of poorly controlled diabetes include damage to the eyes,
kidneys, and nerves, and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.

## Upper Respiratory Tract Infection

Upper respiratory tract infections include infections of the nose, throat,
sinuses, and larynx. Most are viral and self-limited. Symptoms can include
sore throat, cough, nasal congestion, and sneezing.

Rest, fluids, and humidified air help with recovery. Antibiotics are reserved
for bacterial infections, which are suggested by persistent high fever,
purulent discharge, or clinical features such as strep throat confirmed by
testing.

## Acute Bronchitis

Acute bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchial tubes, usually caused by a
viral infection. The hallmark symptom is a cough that may produce mucus.
Symptoms may last up to 3 weeks.

Management is supportive: fluids, rest, and breathing of moist air. Cough
suppressants may be used for bothersome cough. Antibiotics are generally not
indicated for acute bronchitis in otherwise healthy patients.

---

## Gastroenteritis (Stomach Flu)

Gastroenteritis is inflammation of the stomach and intestines, most commonly
caused by a virus or contaminated food. It typically presents with diarrhea,
vomiting, abdominal cramps, and sometimes fever.

The mainstay of treatment is oral rehydration to replace lost fluids and
electrolytes. This can be done with oral rehydration solution or clear
fluids. Most cases resolve within a few days. Seek medical attention for
signs of dehydration, blood in the stool, high fever, or symptoms lasting
more than 3 days in an adult.

## Constipation

Constipation is defined as fewer than three bowel movements per week, often
accompanied by hard, dry stools or difficulty passing stool. Common causes
include low fiber intake, inadequate fluid intake, inactivity, and certain
medications.

Management begins with lifestyle measures: increasing dietary fiber (fruits,
vegetables, whole grains), drinking adequate fluids, and regular exercise. A
patient who does not improve with lifestyle measures may use a gentle laxative
at the lowest effective dose for a short duration.

Red flags requiring evaluation include blood in the stool, unintentional
weight loss, persistent abdominal pain, and a change in bowel habits after
the age of 50.

---

## Insomnia

Insomnia is difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early,
despite adequate opportunity for sleep, with daytime consequences such as
fatigue or poor concentration.

Behavioral measures are the first-line treatment: a consistent sleep schedule,
a dark and quiet bedroom, avoiding caffeine in the afternoon and evening,
limiting screens before bed, and avoiding daytime naps.

Sedative medication should be used only for a short duration and under a
doctor's supervision. Persistent insomnia with mood symptoms or snoring with
breathing pauses should be evaluated by a doctor.

---

## Seasonal Allergies (Allergic Rhinitis)

Allergic rhinitis occurs when the immune system overreacts to airborne
allergens such as pollen, dust mites, or animal dander. Symptoms include
sneezing, a runny or itchy nose, itchy eyes, and nasal congestion.

Management includes avoiding known triggers, using a saline nasal rinse, and
antihistamine medication at the lowest effective dose. Intranasal steroid
sprays are effective for more persistent symptoms. Severe symptoms with
wheezing or anaphylaxis require urgent medical attention.

## Lifestyle and Preventive Care

Routine preventive care is an important part of general medicine. This
includes periodic blood pressure checks, blood sugar screening in adults,
cholesterol testing, and age-appropriate cancer screening.

A balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean protein,
combined with at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week, is
recommended for most adults. Adequate sleep, stress management, limiting
alcohol, and avoiding tobacco are all part of a healthy lifestyle.

Patients should maintain an updated immunization record and discuss
vaccination schedules with their doctor.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Patients should seek urgent medical attention for the following warning
signs:

- Severe chest pain or pressure
- Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
- Sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body
- Difficulty speaking or confusion
- Sudden severe headache
- Loss of consciousness or fainting
- Severe bleeding that does not stop
- Persistent vomiting with inability to keep fluids down
- Signs of dehydration: extreme thirst, dark urine, or dizziness
- Blood in vomit, stool, or urine
- A high fever that does not respond to medication
